Indian cobra

Naja naja · Reptile · Carnivore

The Indian cobra, or spectacled cobra, spreads a hood that carries a mark like a pair of spectacles. It eats rats, frogs and other small animals, and so is often found near grain stores and houses. Its bite is dangerous and needs hospital treatment at once.

What the Indian cobra eats

The Indian cobra eats rats, mice, frogs, toads, lizards, birds' eggs and other snakes. Rats draw it to grain stores, fields and houses, which is how it comes so often into contact with people. It hunts mainly around dusk and in the early morning.

How it lives

When alarmed, a cobra lifts the front of its body, spreads the long ribs of its neck into a hood, sways and hisses. This is a warning, and a cobra left alone will usually move away. The female lays her eggs in a rat hole or termite mound and stays with them until they hatch. The young can spread a hood and bite from the first day.

How to tell it apart

The mark on the back of the hood, shaped like a pair of spectacles, is the best sign. The monocled cobra of eastern and north-eastern India has a single ring. The harmless rat snake, or dhaman, is often killed in mistake for a cobra: it is long and fast, with large eyes and no hood.

The Indian cobra and people

The cobra, or nag, is revered in India and worshipped on Nag Panchami. It is also one of the 'big four' snakes that cause most snakebite deaths in the country. A snake has no outer ears: a snake charmer's cobra follows the movement of the pipe, not the music. Snake charming with wild snakes is banned.

Is it in danger?

The Indian cobra is listed as Least Concern. The species is widespread or numerous enough that it is not considered at risk of extinction at present.
