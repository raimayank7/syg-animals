House mouse

Mus musculus · Mammal · Omnivore

The house mouse has followed people to almost every part of the world. It is active mainly at night, has keen hearing and smell, and breeds very quickly.

What the house mouse eats

Mice prefer grain and seeds but will eat nearly anything, including soap, candle wax and glue. They nibble a little at many places instead of eating one full meal, and spoil far more food than they eat with their droppings and urine.

How it lives

A house mouse is active mostly at night. It runs along walls, feeling the way with its whiskers. It can squeeze through a gap no wider than a pencil. Mice breed very fast: a female can have several litters a year, and the young can breed when a few weeks old. The babies are born pink, blind and hairless.

How to tell it apart

A mouse is small, with large rounded ears, a pointed nose and a thin tail about as long as its body. A young rat can look similar, but it has a heavier head and much bigger feet for its size. A shrew, the chuchundar, is not a mouse at all. It has a long snout and tiny eyes, and it eats insects.

The house mouse and people

Mice have followed people around the world, living in houses, shops and ships. They damage stored food and can spread disease. Tame white mice, on the other hand, are among the most important animals in medical research. In India the mouse is also honoured as the mount of Lord Ganesha.

How it is classified

Scientists sort living things into groups within groups. Reading down, each step is a smaller group, ending with the species itself.

  1. Kingdom Animalia
  2. Phylum Chordata
  3. Class Mammalia
  4. Order Rodentia
  5. Family Muridae
  6. Genus Mus
  7. Species Mus musculus

More distant relatives in the order Rodentia: Indian crested porcupine, Indian palm squirrel, North American beaver.

Classification follows the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy. Other reference works arrange some groups differently. How classification works.

Is it in danger?

The house mouse is listed as Least Concern. The species is widespread or numerous enough that it is not considered at risk of extinction at present.

This is the IUCN Red List category as recorded in Wikidata on 11 November 2026. The IUCN Red List itself is the authority and may carry a newer assessment. What the categories mean.
