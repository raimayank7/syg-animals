Veiled chameleon

Chamaeleo calyptratus · Reptile · Insectivore

Chameleons can move each eye on its own and shoot out a sticky tongue longer than their body to catch insects. They change colour mainly to signal mood and to control body temperature, not simply to match the background.

What the veiled chameleon eats

The chameleon catches insects with its tongue, which shoots out farther than the length of its body in a fraction of a second. The sticky tip grips the insect and pulls it back. Unusually for a chameleon, this species also eats leaves, mainly as a way of getting water in a dry land.

How it lives

Chameleons live alone in bushes and trees and move with a slow, rocking walk, like a leaf in a breeze. Each eye turns by itself, so the animal can look two ways at once. Toes joined into two pads grip twigs like tongs, and the tail curls round branches. It drinks dew and raindrops from leaves, never from pools.

How to tell it apart

A tall, helmet-like crest on the head, the 'veil', marks this species, and it is tallest in males. The body is green with bands of yellow, blue or brown. The Indian chameleon found in India has only a low helmet. Chameleons change colour mostly with mood, temperature and light, and to signal to each other, not to match any background.

The veiled chameleon and people

The veiled chameleon comes from Yemen and south-western Saudi Arabia. It is bred in large numbers as a pet, and escaped animals now live wild in Florida and Hawaii. Chameleons are quite harmless, though in many places people wrongly fear them as poisonous.

How it is classified

Scientists sort living things into groups within groups. Reading down, each step is a smaller group, ending with the species itself.

  1. Kingdom Animalia
  2. Phylum Chordata
  3. Class Reptilia
  4. Order Squamata
  5. Family Chamaeleonidae
  6. Genus Chamaeleo
  7. Species Chamaeleo calyptratus

More distant relatives in the order Squamata: common house gecko, Indian cobra, Indian python, king cobra, Komodo dragon.

Classification follows the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy. Other reference works arrange some groups differently. How classification works.

Is it in danger?

The veiled chameleon is listed as Least Concern. The species is widespread or numerous enough that it is not considered at risk of extinction at present.
