Common octopus

Octopus vulgaris · Invertebrate · Carnivore

An octopus has eight arms lined with suckers, three hearts and blue blood. It is the most intelligent of the invertebrates, can change colour in an instant to hide, and squirts ink to escape.

What the common octopus eats

The octopus eats crabs, lobsters, clams and other shellfish, and fish when it can catch them. It pounces and wraps the prey in its arms, bites with a hard beak like a parrot's that is hidden where the arms meet, and injects saliva that paralyses the animal and softens its flesh. Piles of empty shells mark the entrance to its den.

How it lives

An octopus lives alone in a hole among rocks and hunts mainly at night. It has three hearts and blue blood, and no bones at all, so it can squeeze through any gap wider than its beak. It changes colour and skin texture in an instant to match its surroundings, and squirts a cloud of ink to cover its escape. It can solve puzzles and unscrew jar lids.

How to tell it apart

It has a soft, bag-like body and eight arms, each with two rows of suckers. A squid has a long body with fins, eight arms and two longer tentacles. A cuttlefish has a broad flat body with a fin all round it and a chalky bone inside.

The common octopus and people

The female lays strings of eggs in her den and guards and cleans them for weeks without eating. She dies soon after they hatch. Octopuses are short-lived, usually only a year or two. They are fished and eaten in many countries, and growing knowledge of how intelligent they are has started a debate about how they are treated.

How it is classified

Scientists sort living things into groups within groups. Reading down, each step is a smaller group, ending with the species itself.

  2. Phylum Mollusca

Classification follows the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy. Other reference works arrange some groups differently. How classification works.

Is it in danger?

The common octopus is listed as Least Concern. The species is widespread or numerous enough that it is not considered at risk of extinction at present.
