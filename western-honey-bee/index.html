Western honey bee

Apis mellifera · Invertebrate · Herbivore

Honey bees live in a colony with one queen, many female workers and some male drones. Workers make honey from flower nectar and tell each other where flowers are by dancing. As they feed, bees pollinate many of the crops people eat.

What the Western honey bee eats

Bees feed on nectar and pollen from flowers. Nectar gives energy and pollen gives protein for the growing young. Workers carry nectar home in a special stomach, pass it from bee to bee and fan it with their wings until it thickens into honey. Honey is their food store for times when no flowers are open.

How it lives

A colony has one queen, who lays all the eggs, thousands of female workers and, in season, some male drones. Workers build the comb from wax, nurse the young, guard the hive and gather food. A bee that finds good flowers performs a 'waggle dance' on the comb that tells the others the direction and distance. A worker's sting is barbed, so she dies after stinging a person.

How to tell it apart

A honey bee is slim and golden-brown with darker bands, less hairy than a bumblebee, and carries balls of pollen on its hind legs. A wasp is smooth and shiny with bright yellow and black bands and a narrow waist. India has native honey bees too: the Indian hive bee and the large rock bee that hangs its single comb from cliffs, tall trees and buildings.

The Western honey bee and people

People have kept bees for honey and wax for thousands of years. Their greater value is pollination: many fruit, vegetable, oilseed and nut crops set far more seed and fruit when bees visit. Bees are harmed by pesticides, by mites and disease, and by the loss of wild flowers.

How it is classified

Scientists sort living things into groups within groups. Reading down, each step is a smaller group, ending with the species itself.
