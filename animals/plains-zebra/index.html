Plains zebra

Equus quagga · Mammal · Herbivore

Zebras are wild horses with black and white stripes. Every zebra has its own pattern, like a fingerprint. They live in family groups that join into large herds, and often graze alongside wildebeest.

What the plains zebra eats

Zebras are grazers and can live on tall, coarse grass that many antelopes leave alone. They must drink almost every day, so they are never far from water. On the great migration in East Africa, zebras often go first and eat down the long grass, and the wildebeest follow.

How it lives

The family is one stallion, several mares and their foals, and it stays together for years. Many families join into large herds. A foal can stand within minutes and run within about an hour. It learns its mother's own pattern of stripes, voice and smell in its first days.

How to tell it apart

Every zebra's stripes are different, like fingerprints. The plains zebra has broad stripes that go under the belly, often with faint 'shadow' stripes in between. Grevy's zebra is larger, with narrow stripes, a white belly and big round ears. The mountain zebra has a fold of skin under the throat.

The plains zebra and people

Why zebras have stripes was argued over for a long time. Studies now point to biting flies, which find it hard to land on striped surfaces. Zebras have never been truly tamed, unlike horses and donkeys. Fences and farms block their old routes, but the plains zebra is still the most numerous of the wild horses.

Is it in danger?

The plains zebra is listed as Near Threatened. The species is not yet classed as threatened, but it is close and may become so soon.

This is the IUCN Red List category as recorded in Wikidata on 11 November 2026. The IUCN Red List itself is the authority and may carry a newer assessment. What the categories mean.
