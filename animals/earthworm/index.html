Earthworm

Lumbricus terrestris · Invertebrate · Herbivore

Earthworms tunnel through the soil, swallowing earth and dead leaves. Their burrows let in air and water, and their droppings enrich the soil. They have no eyes or lungs and breathe through their damp skin.

What the earthworm eats

Earthworms eat dead leaves and the rotting plant matter in soil. This species comes up at night, grips a fallen leaf and pulls it down into its burrow to eat later. Soil passes through the gut with the food, and the worm leaves it at the surface as small coiled heaps called casts.

How it lives

An earthworm has no eyes, ears or lungs. It senses light and touch through its skin and breathes through the skin too, which must stay moist. It moves by stretching and shortening its rings, gripping with tiny bristles. Every worm is both male and female, but two must still pair to lay eggs, which are left in a small lemon-shaped cocoon.

How to tell it apart

A long, soft, pinkish-brown body of many rings, with a smooth swollen band nearer the front end, is the sign of a grown earthworm. Cutting a worm in half does not make two worms. At best the head end may live. A millipede has a hard body and many legs. A blind snake, sometimes mistaken for a worm, is dry and shiny, with scales and a tongue that flicks.

The earthworm and people

Charles Darwin studied earthworms for decades and showed how they slowly turn over the whole topsoil. Their burrows let air and water into the ground, and their casts are rich plant food. Farmers and gardeners use worms to turn kitchen and farm waste into vermicompost.
