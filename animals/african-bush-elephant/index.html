African bush elephant

Loxodonta africana · Mammal · Herbivore

The African bush elephant is the largest land animal alive. It has very large ears, shaped a little like the map of Africa, which help it lose heat. Both males and females grow tusks. Herds are led by an old female, the matriarch.

What the African bush elephant eats

An elephant eats grass, leaves, bark, roots and fruit, and spends well over half of each day feeding. It needs a very large amount of food and water daily. Elephants push over trees to reach the leaves and dig in dry riverbeds for water with their feet and trunks. Other animals then drink from the holes they leave.

How it lives

Family herds are made up of related females and their young and are led by the oldest female, the matriarch, who remembers where water can be found in a drought. Males leave in their teens and live alone or in loose groups. Pregnancy lasts about 22 months, the longest of any land animal, and the whole herd helps to guard a calf.

How to tell it apart

Compare it with the Asian elephant: the African has far larger ears, shaped roughly like the map of Africa, a back that dips in the middle, and two 'fingers' at the tip of the trunk where the Asian has one. Both males and females normally grow tusks. The smaller forest elephant of Central Africa is now counted as a separate species.

The African bush elephant and people

Elephants have been killed in huge numbers for the ivory of their tusks. International trade in ivory was banned in 1989, but poaching goes on. As farms spread, elephants raid crops and people are hurt. Fences of beehives and chilli plants are among the simple methods used to keep them out of fields.

Is it in danger?

The African bush elephant is listed as Endangered. The species faces a very high risk of extinction in the wild.

This is the IUCN Red List category as recorded in Wikidata on 11 November 2026. The IUCN Red List itself is the authority and may carry a newer assessment. What the categories mean.
