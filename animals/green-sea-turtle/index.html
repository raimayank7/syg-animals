Green sea turtle

Chelonia mydas · Reptile · Herbivore

The green sea turtle is named for the greenish fat under its shell. Adults graze on seagrass and algae. Females return to the beach region where they hatched to lay their eggs in the sand.

What the green sea turtle eats

Adult green turtles are mostly vegetarian, grazing on seagrass and seaweed. This is unusual among sea turtles. The young eat jellyfish, sponges and other small sea animals before they change to plants. The fat under the shell is tinted green by this diet, and that is where the name comes from. The shell itself is brown or olive.

How it lives

Green turtles spend almost their whole lives at sea but breathe air and must come up for it. A female returns to the region where she hatched, crawls up a sandy beach at night, digs a pit with her flippers and lays around a hundred eggs. The hatchlings dig out together weeks later and run for the sea. Warm nests produce more females.

How to tell it apart

It has a smooth, heart-shaped shell, a small blunt head and a single pair of scales between the eyes. The olive ridley is smaller, with a rounder, greyish-olive shell. The hawksbill has a narrow head with a hooked beak and overlapping shell plates. The leatherback is huge, with a ridged, leathery back and no hard shell.

The green sea turtle and people

In India green turtles nest in Gujarat, Lakshadweep and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Sea turtles drown in fishing nets, swallow plastic bags that look like jellyfish and lose nesting beaches to building. Bright lights behind a beach draw hatchlings inland, away from the sea.

Is it in danger?

The green sea turtle is listed as Least Concern. The species is widespread or numerous enough that it is not considered at risk of extinction at present.

This is the IUCN Red List category as recorded in Wikidata on 11 November 2026. The IUCN Red List itself is the authority and may carry a newer assessment. What the categories mean.
