Whale shark

Rhincodon typus · Fish · Carnivore

The whale shark is the largest fish in the world. It is harmless to people and feeds by filtering plankton and small fish from the water. Each one has its own pattern of pale spots. They visit the coast of Gujarat every year.

What the whale shark eats

The largest fish in the sea feeds on some of the smallest food: plankton, fish eggs, krill and tiny fish. It swims slowly with its huge mouth open, or hangs upright and gulps, and strains the food from the water with pads in its gills. Its thousands of teeth are tiny and are not used for feeding.

How it lives

Whale sharks roam warm seas alone, and gather in numbers where food is rich, for example where fish or corals spawn. They often swim near the surface. The female keeps her eggs inside her body until they hatch, and gives birth to live young. They are thought to live for many decades.

How to tell it apart

A broad flat head with the mouth right at the front, and a dark back covered in pale spots and stripes like a checked cloth, identify it. Every whale shark's pattern is different. It is a shark, not a whale: it is a fish that breathes with gills, and its tail is upright. The basking shark, another giant plankton-eater, has no spots.

The whale shark and people

It is harmless to people and lets divers swim beside it. Whale sharks visit the coast of Gujarat, where they were once hunted for liver oil. India gave them full protection in 2001, and a campaign among the fishing communities of Saurashtra taught crews to cut their nets and free any that are caught.

How it is classified

Scientists sort living things into groups within groups. Reading down, each step is a smaller group, ending with the species itself.

Classification follows the GBIF Backbone Taxonomy. Other reference works arrange some groups differently. How classification works.

Is it in danger?

The whale shark is listed as Endangered. The species faces a very high risk of extinction in the wild.

This is the IUCN Red List category as recorded in Wikidata on 11 November 2026. The IUCN Red List itself is the authority and may carry a newer assessment. What the categories mean.
