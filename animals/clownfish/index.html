Clownfish

Amphiprion ocellaris · Fish · Omnivore

The clownfish is orange with white bands. It lives among the stinging arms of a sea anemone, protected by a layer of slime on its skin. The fish gets a safe home, and the anemone gets cleaned and defended.

What the clownfish eats

Clownfish eat tiny animals and algae drifting past, and also scraps left over from the meals of the sea anemone they live in. They never go far from the anemone to feed, and dart back into its tentacles at any sign of danger.

How it lives

The anemone's tentacles sting other fish, but a coat of slime on the clownfish's skin protects it. In return the fish chases away animals that would eat the anemone and keeps it clean. All clownfish start life as males. The largest fish in a group is the female, and if she dies, the biggest male changes sex and takes her place.

How to tell it apart

It is bright orange with three white bands, each edged with a thin black line. The orange clownfish, a very close relative, has thicker black edges to its bands. Other anemonefish are brown, black or pink, some with only one or two bands. The male guards the eggs, which are laid on a rock beside the anemone.

The clownfish and people

The 2003 film Finding Nemo made this fish famous and greatly raised the demand for it in aquariums. Most clownfish sold today are bred in tanks, which spares wild reefs. In the sea its future depends on coral reefs and their anemones, and warming water harms both.

Is it in danger?

The clownfish is listed as Least Concern. The species is widespread or numerous enough that it is not considered at risk of extinction at present.
