House spider

Tegenaria domestica · Invertebrate · Carnivore

Spiders are not insects: they have eight legs and two body parts. The house spider spins a sheet-like web in quiet corners and waits in a funnel at one side. It is harmless to people and eats flies and other household insects.

What the house spider eats

House spiders eat flies, mosquitoes, moths, cockroach young and other small insects. The spider waits in its retreat with its feet on the web, and when an insect lands it feels the vibration, rushes out and bites. Spiders cannot chew: they turn the inside of the prey to liquid and suck it up.

How it lives

This spider spins a flat sheet of silk in a quiet corner, with a funnel-shaped tube at the back where it hides. The web is not sticky: insects trip on the tangle of threads. Females stay on the web and may live for several years. Males wander in search of mates when they are grown, and those are the spiders that people find in baths and sinks.

How to tell it apart

A spider is not an insect. It has eight legs, a body in two parts and no wings or antennae, while an insect has six legs and a body in three parts. This species is brownish with a pattern of pale marks on the abdomen and long legs. The cellar spider, another common house guest, has a tiny body on very long thin legs and makes a messy web under ceilings.

The house spider and people

House spiders are harmless to people and catch many insects that are a nuisance indoors. A spider that must go can be put outside with a glass and a card. The untidy grey 'cobwebs' in old rooms are abandoned webs that have gathered dust.
